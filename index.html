Section 1

How to read the data: revenue, units, and ASP

Cobalt surfaces three core metrics for benchmarking. Each answers a different question — and the real insight comes from reading them together, not in isolation.

Revenue
How much is this brand generating?

Total sales dollars. Tells you scale and overall position, but not efficiency or demand. Revenue can grow while share shrinks if the market grows faster.

Units Sold
How much demand is this brand capturing?

Volume of products sold — the demand signal, independent of price. A brand losing units while holding revenue is raising prices to compensate.

ASP
How is this brand competing on price?

Average selling price. Reveals pricing strategy and market position. A rising ASP with declining units suggests trading volume for margin.

Three signals to watch for

As you look at these metrics across Cobalt features, you're watching for one of three situations. A detailed reference table with all metric combinations is in Section 6 — once you've seen your own numbers, that table will make more sense.

Signal What you're seeing What it means
Gaining share Your revenue and/or units growing faster than the category You're outpacing the market — competitors are losing ground to you
Watch Revenue looks okay but units are declining, or you're growing slower than the category Surface-level numbers can hide a share loss — dig into unit trends and compare to category growth rate
Losing share Revenue or units declining while the category grows, or share % falling over time Competitors are capturing growth you're not — this is the pattern worth investigating in Topic 3
Trend matters more than a single number

Look at 3–6 months before drawing conclusions. Growing revenue in a fast-growing market can still mean losing share. One slow month is noise; six consecutive months of declining unit share is a signal.

Market Analysis uses segment data — a filtered, tighter ASIN pool with its own sales estimation model. Home page, Category Performance, and AI Analyst all use Category Performance data — every ASIN in the subcategory, including inactive or miscategorized products. Same subcategory, different scope, different numbers. See the Appendix for a full explanation.

Section 3
Market Analysis

Your segment view

Market Analysis is where your segments live — and where you'll do your deepest benchmarking work. Because your segment was built with specific filters (brands, keywords, minimum review counts), the data here reflects your actual competitive set, not every product Amazon has in that subcategory.

This precision matters. The Men's Disposable Razors subcategory includes hundreds of ASINs at wildly different price points, review counts, and quality levels — many of which are not competing for the same shopper you are. Your segment, filtered to the brands and product types you actually compete with, gives you a benchmark that means something.

The six tabs in Market Analysis follow a natural analysis progression that moves from market-level to root cause: start with the Overview to understand where you stand and what the market looks like. Move to Brands to see which specific brands are causing the share shifts you observed. Go to Products to see which products from those brands are doing the work. Use Categories if your segment spans multiple subcategories. Then Attributes and Pricing start revealing what those products have in common — the features, configurations, and price points that are connecting with buyers. You don't need all six every time, but this is the logical path from "what's happening?" to "why is it happening?"

In Topic 1, we built four segments at different levels of specificity: Shaving Razors (broadest) → Disposable Razors → and then two at the same level: Women's Disposable and Men's Disposable. For benchmarking your competitive position in Men's Disposable Razors specifically, you'd use the Men's Disposable segment. The broader parent segments are useful for category context but show a larger pool than your true competitive set for that specific subcategory.

Date range note: Market Analysis only offers preset date ranges — no custom date entry. "Last 90 days" in Market Analysis resolves to the last 90 days of available segment data, on roughly a 1-week lag. This will not match "Last 90 days" in Category Performance or AI Analyst exactly, since each feature's date presets anchor to different data update schedules. For the closest comparison, use the same preset across features and note that the actual windows may differ by a few weeks. See the Appendix for more on data lag by feature.

Once you're in your segment, Market Analysis organizes data across six tabs. Here's what each one is for and when to use it.

Overview tab
Primary
Use this when: you want your top-line market share and a quick read on how all brands in your segment compare

The Overview tab is your starting point. It shows your brand's share of the total segment — not the full subcategory, but the competitive set you defined. This is the number that answers "where do I stand among my actual competitors?"

At the top of the page, your market share percentage is displayed prominently alongside total segment revenue for the period. This share is calculated only across the ASINs in your segment — so if your segment is BIC, Gillette, Kitsch, and Schick in Men's Disposable Razors, your share is your revenue as a percentage of those four brands combined, not the full subcategory.

📷 Screenshot needed — Image 2.1 Overview tab showing BIC's market share % prominently displayed. Callout on the share percentage and total segment revenue figure. Note that date range used is Last 90 Days.

Below the headline share number, the Segment Composition brand comparison table lets you switch between three views using the metric selector:

Revenue view

Shows total revenue for each brand in the segment for the selected period. Use this to understand the revenue gap between you and your competitors — who's generating more, who's generating less, and by how much.

📷 Screenshot needed — Image 2.2.1 Brand comparison table with Revenue selected. Brands listed with their revenue figures. Callout on BIC's row and the metric selector toggle.

Market Share view

Shows each brand's share of the segment as a percentage. This is the most direct competitive comparison — you can see at a glance who owns the most share and how yours compares. Track this number over time; consistent share gain or loss is your most important competitive signal.

📷 Screenshot needed — Image 2.2.2 Brand comparison table with Market Share selected. Share percentages shown per brand. Callout on BIC's share and the comparison to top competitor.

Units view

Shows total units sold per brand. Compare units alongside revenue to understand whether competitors are growing through volume or pricing. A brand growing revenue without growing units is raising prices; a brand growing units without growing revenue is likely discounting.

📷 Screenshot needed — Image 2.2.3 Brand comparison table with Units selected. Unit counts per brand. Callout on the difference between a brand with high units/lower revenue vs. high revenue/lower units.

Average Selling Price (ASP)

The ASP view shows each brand's average selling price for the period. Use this to understand where you sit in the price stack relative to your competitors. A significant gap between your ASP and a growing competitor's ASP tells you something about how they're competing — volume at lower prices, or premium positioning at higher prices.

📷 Screenshot needed — Image 2.3 ASP chart showing your brand's ASP vs. segment ASP over time. (Note: this view was not loading at time of writing — update with screenshot when available.)
🪒 Razor example — Overview tab

In the Men's Disposable Razors segment, BIC's share of the segment is 14.9% with total segment revenue of $38.75M. The segment includes all brands that met your filter criteria — BIC, Gillette, Kitsch, and Schick are the top brands you'll see most prominently in screenshots and charts; others appear further down or are grouped into an "other" category in visualizations. This 14.9% is meaningfully different from BIC's 6.27% share of the full Men's Disposable Shaving Razors subcategory — because the denominator is the filtered competitive set, not every ASIN Amazon has categorized in that subcategory including inactive listings and low-quality products. In the revenue view, you can see Gillette's dominant position; in the units view, you can start to see whether Kitsch's growth is volume-driven or price-driven.

Brands tab
Primary
Use this when: you want to see which brands are gaining or losing share over time and when those shifts happened

The Brands tab gives you the time-series view of your competitive set. While the Overview tab shows a snapshot for the selected period, the Brands tab lets you scroll through a stacked area chart to see how each brand's share has moved month by month. As one brand's band grows, another shrinks — making it easy to see which specific brands are responsible for share movement in the market.

Set the chart to Market Share using the metric selector in the top right. The stacked area chart shows each brand as a colored band. You can also switch to Revenue or Units to see those trend lines. Hover over any point to see exact figures for that month.

This tab is where you start connecting market-level trends to brand-level causes. If the Overview shows the market grew 15% but your share dropped, the Brands tab tells you which brand captured that growth and approximately when it started happening.

📷 Screenshot needed — Image 2.4 Brands tab showing stacked area chart with Market Share selected. Each brand in a distinct color. Callout on BIC's shrinking band and a competitor's growing band over the time period.
🪒 Razor example — Brands tab

With Market Share selected on the Brands tab, you can see BIC's share of the segment declining steadily over the period while Kitsch's band grows — brand-level share shifting from one to another. This directly answers "am I moving in the right direction?" and shows you exactly which brand is capturing the share BIC is losing. Noting when the shift started helps you correlate to external events: a competitor's pricing change, a new product launch, a promotional period. From here, the next question is how Kitsch is doing it — which takes you to the Products and Pricing tabs.

Pricing tab
Primary
Use this when: you want to understand how your pricing compares to competitors and where your revenue sits in the price stack

The Pricing tab surfaces several views, the most directly useful for benchmarking being the ASP trend and the price band breakdown. Understanding pricing context is important because ASP movements explain a lot of what you see in revenue and unit numbers — a brand losing units but growing revenue is almost always raising price, and the Pricing tab confirms it.

ASP Trend — your brand vs. segment ASP

This time-series chart shows your brand's ASP over the selected period alongside the segment's average ASP. The gap between the two lines tells you whether you're priced above or below your competitive set — and whether that gap is widening or narrowing. A widening gap where your ASP is rising while the segment holds flat may explain unit share loss.

Your Brand Revenue by Price Band

This view breaks your revenue into price tiers and shows how much of your revenue comes from each band alongside your share within that band. It's the segment-level equivalent of the AI Analyst's Price Positioning section — but here you're comparing against your filtered competitive set, not the full subcategory.

Products tab
Secondary
Use this when: you want to see which specific products are driving the brand-level trends you spotted in the Brands tab

Once you've identified which brands are gaining or losing share, the Products tab lets you go one level deeper — which products from those brands are actually making the moves. The stacked area chart shows the top 5, 10, or 55 products by revenue, each as a colored band. Switch between Revenue, Units, and Market Share to understand whether a brand's growth is coming from one breakout product or distributed across their catalog.

This is where you start answering the "how are they doing it?" question — before you can understand strategy (pricing, keywords, assortment), you need to know which products are responsible.

📷 Screenshot needed — Image 2.5 Products tab with stacked area chart, Revenue selected, top 10 products shown. Each product in a distinct color. Callout on a competitor product whose band is growing.
🪒 Razor example — Products tab

If the Brands tab showed Kitsch gaining share from BIC, the Products tab tells you whether that's coming from a single Kitsch product that's breaking out or from broad growth across their catalog. If it's one product — a specific pack size or style — that's a very different signal than if Kitsch is growing evenly across everything they sell. That distinction shapes what you look at next.

Categories tab
Reference
Use this when: your segment spans multiple subcategories and you want to see how revenue breaks down across them

The Categories tab shows revenue broken down by category node for the products in your segment. It's most useful when you're working with a broader parent segment that spans multiple subcategories — for example, the Shaving Razors segment that includes disposable, cartridge, and other subcategory types. You can see which subcategory is driving the most revenue and how that has shifted over time.

At the narrowest subcategory level (like the Men's Disposable segment), this tab shows limited additional breakdown since all products are already in the same node.

📷 Screenshot needed — Image 2.6 Categories tab showing subcategory revenue breakdown bar chart from the broader Shaving Razors segment. Each subcategory in a distinct color.
Attributes tab
Reference
Use this when: you want to understand what product characteristics are driving revenue within your segment — blade count, pack size, material, and more

The Attributes tab shows how different product attributes map to revenue within your segment. Available attributes depend on what data Cobalt has for the products in your segment — you might see Item Package Quantity, blade count, skin type, or other characteristics. Each attribute type shows a bar chart breaking down revenue and units by that attribute value.

This is where the analysis starts to move from brand-level to product-level patterns — understanding whether high-blade-count products or multi-pack offerings are taking an outsized share of the category. Useful for assortment decisions: should you add or remove SKUs at certain product configurations to better match where demand is concentrated?

In the Men's Disposable Razors segment, BIC's Price Band view shows 34% of revenue concentrated in the $15–$25 tier — over three times the category mix for that band (10.8%). Meanwhile the $25+ tier, where BIC captures just 7.8% of its revenue, grew 107% in the broader category. Putting Market Analysis together

The six tabs follow a natural analysis progression: Overview tells you where the market stands and where you sit within it. Brands shows which brands are causing the share shifts you saw in Overview. Products reveals which specific products from those brands are doing the work. Categories helps if your segment spans multiple subcategories. Attributes starts to show the product-level patterns — what configurations are winning. Pricing connects it back to the revenue and ASP story. You don't need to work through all six every time — start with Overview and Brands to answer the core benchmarking questions, then go deeper in Products and Pricing when you've identified something worth investigating. Topic 3 is where you take that investigation further: who's disrupting the category, how, and what it means for you.

Section 4
Home page & Category Performance

Your category-level view

The Home page and Category Performance use Category Performance data — every ASIN in the subcategory — so numbers will differ from your segment. These views give you a fast read on your position against the full market and are useful for a quick share check or understanding your brand in the context of the broader category landscape.

Home page — Market Share card and Sales Breakdown table

The Market Share card at the top of the Home page shows your share across your top 5 categories, using your brand filter and date range selection. Use the filters at the top of the page (Country, Category, Brand) to focus the data — especially if you have multiple brands integrated.

Important: the Market Share card uses the date range selected at the top of the page, which is on a 72-hour lag from Seller/Vendor Central. The numbers here are a quick read, but for a more accurate market share comparison, go to the Sales Breakdown by Category table below it.

📷 Screenshot needed — Image 3.1 Home page Market Share card. Callout on: filter selectors (Country/Category/Brand), the share % displayed, and the "Top 5 categories only" note.

The Sales Breakdown by Category table normalizes the date range to account for the Category Performance data source being on a 3-week lag — so the date range shown at the bottom right of the table will be different from the rest of the page. This is intentional: it means you're comparing your sales data and category performance data for the same actual window, giving you a more accurate share figure. Note the date range label in the bottom right of the table.

Expand any category row to see your share broken down by subcategory. For the razor example, you can drill into Women's Disposable and Men's Disposable separately to see your share at each level.

Category Performance — Benchmark Mode

Navigate to Category Performance in the left nav and select your subcategory. You can also get there directly by clicking on any subcategory from the Home page Sales Breakdown table — it will open Category Performance pre-filtered to that subcategory.

In Benchmark Mode, filter to your brand using the brand selector at the top. You'll see your brand's revenue, units, and ASP compared to the subcategory average side by side — not against individual named competitors, but against the aggregate. Benchmark Mode is useful for understanding whether you're above or below the category's growth rate on each metric.

You can also navigate up and down the category tree to see your share at different levels. Going up to Shaving & Hair Removal Products shows your position in the broader parent category; staying at Men's Disposable Shaving Razors shows your share in that specific subcategory.

🪒 Razor example — Home page and Category Performance

On the Home page Sales Breakdown table, BIC's share of Men's Disposable Shaving Razors shows the full-subcategory view — this is a different number than BIC's 14.9% segment share, because the denominator is all ASINs in the subcategory, not just the four brands in the segment. When to use this vs. Market Analysis

Use the Home page for a weekly pulse check — your share at a glance. Use Category Performance Benchmark Mode when you want to compare your metrics to the full category average. Use Market Analysis when you need to understand exactly how you compare to the specific brands you compete with.

Section 5
AI Analyst — Benchmark Brand

Your automated benchmark report

The AI Analyst's Benchmark Brand workflow generates a structured benchmark report for your brand in a selected subcategory — no manual data pulling required. It uses Category Performance data and is designed for orientation: here's where you stand, here's what's moving, here's what it means. Use it to get oriented quickly, then go deeper in Market Analysis.

How to run the report

1

Navigate to AI Analyst

In the left nav, click AI Analyst. Select Benchmark Brand from the workflow options.

2

Configure your report

Select your Marketplace, your Brand, and the Subcategory to benchmark against. The category picker shows subcategories where your brand has products. You can only select one subcategory per report.

3

Run Analysis

Click Run Analysis. The report takes a few minutes to generate — Cobalt fetches the category data, runs an AI analysis, and assembles a formatted report.

📷 Screenshot needed — Image 5.2 Benchmark Brand configuration modal showing Marketplace, Brand (BIC), and Category (Men's Disposable Shaving Razors) selectors.

What the report covers

Brand Performance

Your market share % and trend over time vs. total category revenue. YoY deltas for your revenue, units, and ASP alongside category figures. This is your orientation section — where you stand and which direction you're moving.

Competitive Landscape

Top brands by revenue, current vs. prior period. Scatter plot of revenue vs. average buybox price. Brand comparison table with revenue, units, and ASP trend indicators per brand.

Price Positioning

Your revenue distribution by price band vs. the category's distribution. Your share within each band. Risk and Opportunity labels where your concentration is in a declining or growing tier.

Search Insights

Coming soon. Will include keyword and search demand analysis.

Limitations to know

  • One subcategory at a time — you can't combine subcategories the way a segment can. If your brand spans multiple subcategories, run a separate report for each.
  • Category Performance data — uses the all-ASIN data set, not your segment. Numbers will differ from Market Analysis.
  • No ASIN-level detail — brand-level only. You can see that a competitor grew revenue but not which products drove it.
  • No export — currently view-only in-app. Use screenshots for sharing.
  • No drilldown within the report — to investigate a finding, go to Market Analysis, Category Performance, or other Cobalt tools.
🪒 Razor example — AI Analyst

Running Benchmark Brand for BIC in Men's Disposable Shaving Razors surfaces the key orientation data in minutes: 6.27% market share (down 1.09pp), $2.91M revenue (down 1.37%), 233K units (down 24%), ASP $12.45 (up 30%) — all while category revenue grew 15.71%. The Price Positioning section flags that BIC concentrates 34% of its revenue in the $15–$25 tier while that tier holds only 10.8% of category revenue, and BIC is losing share there. Meanwhile the $25+ tier — where BIC captures just 7.8% of its revenue — grew 107%. This is the orientation. What the report doesn't tell you: which specific ASINs or competitors are driving that $25+ growth, or whether BIC's unit decline is concentrated in one product. That's where Market Analysis takes over.

How to get the most out of it

Use Benchmark Brand to identify the questions worth asking. Then bring those questions into Market Analysis. The report tells you something is happening; your segment helps you understand why and against whom.

Section 6

Side-by-side: the razor example across methods

The same question — how is BIC performing in Men's Disposable Razors? — produces different answers depending on where you look in Cobalt, because the underlying data sets are different. Here's what each method tells you and what it leaves unanswered.

Category Performance / AI Analyst

What it tells you: BIC holds 6.27% of the full Men's Disposable Shaving Razors subcategory — down 1.09pp while the category grew 15.71%. Units fell 24%; ASP rose 30%. BIC is losing share in a growing market, compensating with price.

What it doesn't tell you: How BIC performs specifically against the products and brands vying for the same buyer shopping for a specific type of razor. No ASIN-level detail. No ability to narrow the pool to your true competitive set.

Best next action: Use the price band data to understand where BIC's revenue is concentrated vs. where the category is growing. Note the unit decline pattern. Check where BIC is over- or under-indexed vs. the category, then go to Market Analysis to investigate which competitors are capturing the share BIC is losing.

Market Analysis (Segment)

What it tells you: BIC holds 14.9% of the segment — your custom view narrowed to the products and brands you are going head-to-head with for a sale. This is a very different number from 6.27% because the denominator reflects your actual competitive set. The trend view shows when specific brand share shifts started happening.

What it doesn't tell you: How BIC fits within the full subcategory picture — for that, Category Performance and AI Analyst give you the broader market context.

Best next action: Identify which brand is gaining share fastest in your segment. Then dig into how they're doing it — are they adding more ASINs, expanding into different styles or price points, growing a single breakout product, or accumulating reviews faster than you? Understanding the mechanism sets up Topic 3, which focuses entirely on identifying what's driving disruption and what to do about it.

↑ Up ↓ Down ↑ Up Category units ↑ Revenue held by price increases while demand declines — category growing but you're losing unit share. Pricing is masking a volume problem. Watch
↑ Up ↑ Up ↓ Down Category ASP ↑ Gaining units through lower pricing while market moves up — volume play that may cede premium positioning Watch
↑ Up → Flat ↑ Up Category rev ↑↑ Revenue growing but slower than the market — share is eroding even though the absolute number looks fine Watch
↓ Down ↓ Down ↑ Up Category rev ↑ Losing volume and raising price while the category grows — common precursor to larger share loss. This is BIC's pattern. Losing share
↓ Down ↓ Down ↓ Down Category stable Declining across all dimensions while the market holds — share erosion on price, volume, and revenue simultaneously Losing share
↓ Down ↑ Up ↓ Down Category ASP ↑ Heavy discounting to defend volume while revenue and margins fall — market is moving up-market, you're moving down Losing share
→ Flat ↓ Down ↑ Up Category units ↑ Revenue flat only because price increases offset unit declines — demand is weakening while the category captures more volume Losing share

These patterns apply whether you're reading Market Analysis, the AI Analyst report, or Category Performance Benchmark Mode. The interpretation framework is the same across data sources — the numbers will differ, but the logic holds.

Appendix

Why numbers differ across features

If the same subcategory shows different numbers in different Cobalt features, that's expected. Two different data sources are in use, each designed for a different level of precision.

Category Performance data
Used by: Home page, Category Performance, AI Analyst
  • Every ASIN in the subcategory
  • Includes inactive, low-volume, and miscategorized products
  • Broad view of the full subcategory
  • ~3-week data lag
  • Consistent across features that use it
Segment data
Used by: Market Analysis
  • Built from your filtered ASIN pool — not a raw category pull
  • Excludes products that don't meet your filter criteria
  • Uses a different sales estimation model than Category Performance
  • ~1-week data lag
  • More precise; customizable to variant and attribute level

Numbers will always differ between these two sources for the same subcategory. The gap is larger when your segment has tight filters and smaller when it's broad. Neither number is "wrong" — they're answering different questions about different versions of the market.
